# Texas ballot, November 3, 2026

Notes on candidates David has asked about ahead of the general election on
Tuesday, November 3, 2026. Started October 17, 2026 from "tell me about Jimmy
Blackrock, who's running for Texas Supreme Court Justice" — the name is
Blacklock.

## Supreme Court of Texas, Place 1 — Chief Justice

**Jimmy Blacklock (R), incumbent.** Chief Justice since January 2025, when
Governor Abbott elevated him after Nathan Hecht hit the mandatory judicial
retirement age. Abbott first appointed him to the court in 2018; he won
election to the seat afterward. Yale Law School. Before the bench he was
Abbott's general counsel and worked in the Texas Attorney General's office,
where he handled challenges to the Affordable Care Act and defended Texas
abortion law.

A self-described originalist. Wrote the August 2021 opinion holding that the
Texas Constitution lets each legislative chamber compel absent members to
attend, by physical arrest if needed — the ruling in the fight over House
Democrats leaving the state to break quorum. In late 2024 he wrote a strongly
worded opinion siding with a Texas judge who refused to perform same-sex
weddings on religious grounds.

Ran unopposed in the March 3, 2026 Republican primary.

**Maggie Ellis (D), challenger.** Justice on the Texas Third Court of Appeals
in Austin, elected to it in 2024. Practiced representing individuals before
taking the bench; board certified in juvenile law. Beat Cory Carlyle in the
March 3, 2026 Democratic primary.

## Where and when to vote

Asked October 19, 2026: "where do I go for early voting and when is it open?"

**Early voting: Monday October 19 through Friday October 30, 2026.** Every
Travis County early voting site is open 7 a.m. to 7 p.m. every day of that
window, weekends included (Saturday October 24 and Sunday October 25). Seven
sites stay open until 10 p.m. on the last two days, October 29 and 30 — Ben Hur
Shrine Center, Lakeway Activity Center, Millennium Youth Entertainment Complex,
PfISD Rock Gym, Randalls Brodie, the Travis County Clerk's office and the UT
Texas Union. The Manor site is not one of them.

**Nearest site: Manor ISD Administration Building**, Technology Room, 10335
Highway 290, Manor 78653.

During early voting any registered Travis County voter may vote at any early
voting site in the county, so the Manor building is the convenient choice rather
than the required one.

**Election day is Tuesday November 3, 2026**, polls 7 a.m. to 7 p.m. Anyone in
line when the polls close may still vote.

The current list of sites and wait times is at votetravis.gov.

## Manor ISD bond, Propositions A and B

Asked October 21, 2026: "how much will the 2026 school district bond cost us above
our baseline debt obligations?"

**On the ballot.** Two propositions, called unanimously by the Manor ISD board for
November 3, 2026.

- **Proposition A, $391.5 million principal.** Manor New Tech High School expansion,
  a Bluebonnet Trail Elementary rebuild, Manor High School renovation and location
  consolidation, a theater addition and an 18+ program facility, renovations at Decker
  and Blake Manor elementaries, districtwide safety and security, new buses. With
  interest, $701,466,608.
- **Proposition B, $8.5 million principal.** Student and staff technology. With
  interest, $8,825,374.

Together $400 million borrowed and **$710.3 million repaid**, of which about
**$310 million is interest**.

**Above the baseline.** Manor ISD already owes $624,834,706 in tax-supported debt,
carried by an I&S (debt service) rate of **$0.3701 per $100**. The board adopted that
rate in August 2026 deliberately above what the existing debt needs, so the surplus
can defease or refund old bonds and cut long-term interest; the 2026-27 total rate is
$1.0755, being $0.7054 M&O plus $0.3701 I&S, down from $1.0814.

The district's own estimate is that passing both propositions **raises the rate by
3.85 cents per $100** — essentially all of it Proposition A, since Proposition B
rounds to zero. That 3.85 cents is the answer to "above baseline": **$38.50 a year
per $100,000 of taxable value**, for as long as the new debt is outstanding.

**What it costs this household**, at the values David carries in YNAB as of October
2026 and at the $140,000 school homestead exemption Texas voters approved in
November 2025:

| | Taxable value | The bond's share |
|---|---|---|
| Home, less the $140,000 homestead exemption | $209,000 | $80 a year |
| Second house, no homestead exemption | $314,500 | $121 a year |
| **Both** | | **$202 a year, about $17 a month** |

Over a thirty-year repayment at today's values that is roughly **$6,000** — more if
appraisals rise, less if they fall.

**Read the district's headline carefully.** Manor ISD advertises a *decrease* of $9.56
a month for the average home, which it puts at $331,764, if both propositions pass.
That is a year-over-year comparison and not the cost of the bond: appraised values fell
this year, and the district nets the fall against the increase. The bond's own cost is
the 3.85 cents, paid whether or not a particular home's appraisal went down. Homeowners
65 and older or disabled who hold the Over-65 exemption, now $200,000, pay nothing more
provided they make no major improvements.

Sources: manorisd.net Bond 2026 (overview, Finances & Estimated Impact, FAQ); Manor ISD
Board Briefs, August 17, 2026; Texas Scorecard, "Manor ISD Asks Taxpayers to Back $710
Million in Bond Debt."

### How much bigger does the bond make the district's debt?

Asked October 21, 2026: "in percentages how much higher is the district's debt
obligation if the bond passes, compared to their current debt obligation of
outstanding bonds? Not what they've been collecting but what they would have had
to collect at a minimum." So this compares obligations — what must be repaid,
principal and interest — and not the I&S revenue the district has been taking in.

| | Principal | Interest | Must be repaid |
|---|---|---|---|
| Outstanding bonds, per the district's own figure | — | — | $624,834,706 |
| Proposition A | $391,500,000 | $309,966,608 | $701,466,608 |
| Proposition B | $8,500,000 | $325,374 | $8,825,374 |
| **After both pass** | | | **$1,335,126,688** |

**The obligation rises about 114 percent** — the new debt alone, $710.3 million,
is 1.14 times everything still owed on every bond outstanding. Put the other way,
the district's total debt obligation slightly more than doubles, to about $1.34
billion. That is why Texas Scorecard writes that Proposition A would "more than
double" the district's tax-funded debt.

**The figure this turns on.** $624,834,706 is the number the district's own
documentation carries for current tax-supported debt, and reading it as principal
plus interest is what makes Scorecard's "more than double" arithmetic work. A
second figure is in circulation — nearly $741 million owed, of which nearly $252
million is interest, which looks like an earlier snapshot of the same obligation
taken before the June 2025 defeasance of $26.9 million and the $41 million of
refunding savings. On that basis the increase is 96 percent rather than 114. The
Texas Bond Review Board's last closed year, FY2025, shows $473,964,999 of
tax-supported principal outstanding and no new debt issued that year.

Either basis gives the same plain answer: **passing the bond roughly doubles what
Manor ISD owes.**

**And a minimum, not a ceiling.** The $310 million of interest is the district's
estimate at the rates it assumes; the ballot language commits only the $400
million of principal, and bonds sold into worse markets cost more. The annual
side, for scale: the 2026 tax rate worksheet puts the debt service owed this year
on existing bonds at $42,616,423, against an I&S levy of $0.3701 per $100 on
about $11.7 billion of certified value.

### The rate as a percentage, and the all-in cost of the $400 million

Asked October 21, 2026: "what's the percentage impact to our tax rate and also
what's the total cost of the $400 million bond after you account for interest and
anything else?"

**The rate.** The 3.85 cents is a **3.58 percent** increase in the Manor ISD tax
rate: $1.0755 to $1.1140 per $100. All of it lands on the debt-service half, so the
I&S rate alone rises **10.40 percent**, $0.3701 to $0.4086. Measured against the
whole property tax bill the share is smaller: his second house paid $9,858 in 2025
on $314,500, a 3.13 percent all-in rate across county, hospital district, college,
MUD and school, and the bond's $121 of that is about **1.2 percent** of the bill.

| Against | Now | After | Increase |
|---|---|---|---|
| Manor ISD total rate | $1.0755 | $1.1140 | 3.58% |
| I&S (debt) rate only | $0.3701 | $0.4086 | 10.40% |
| His whole tax bill | | | about 1.2% |

**The total cost.** $400 million borrowed is **$710.3 million repaid** — $310.3
million of it interest, **1.78 times the principal**, on the district's own
estimates ($701,466,608 on Proposition A and $8,825,374 on Proposition B).

Three things sit outside that number:

- **Issuance costs**, roughly **$3 to $4 million** — underwriter discount, bond
  counsel, municipal advisor, rating fees — are paid out of the proceeds rather than
  added on top, so under $397 million actually reaches the buildings. Manor ISD has
  not published a figure; recent Texas ISD official statements run 0.75 to 1.0
  percent of par (Galena Park 2026, $959,000 on $110 million; Dallas ISD, $3.82
  million on $500 million).
- **The interest is an estimate, not a cap.** The ballot commits only the $400
  million of principal. Bonds sold into higher rates cost more, and the district
  plans to issue in series over years, so the final interest is unknown on election
  day.
- **Operating costs.** New and expanded campuses cost money to staff, cool and
  maintain, and that falls on the M&O rate, which bond money may not pay. Nothing in
  the $710.3 million covers it.

For this household the bond's own share is about $202 a year, roughly $6,000 over a
thirty-year repayment at today's values.

### The I&S rate with the bond, against a true no-bond floor

Asked October 21, 2026: "compare what our I&S rate would be if the bond didn't pass
and we only paid our minimum current debt obligations versus if it did pass."

The comparison he wants is not 0.3701 against 0.4086, because **0.3701 is itself a
choice**. Senate Bill 1453, effective January 1, 2026, makes a district calculate the
*minimum debt service rate* — enough to pay only the bond payments due that year,
defeasance and refinancing excluded — and allows a higher rate only on a specific
motion carried by a 60 percent supermajority. Manor ISD took that vote on August 17,
2026, unanimously, and Trustee Patterson's motion said the excess "will be used for
the payment of bond debt through defeasance or refunding to reduce the long term
interest cost on currently existing debt." The district posted only the
higher-debt-rate worksheet; it has not published the minimum figure.

| | I&S rate per $100 | What it pays for |
|---|---|---|
| Minimum on existing debt, bond fails | **about $0.31** (estimated) | this year's principal and interest, nothing more |
| Adopted now, bond fails | $0.3701 | that, plus about $6.5M a year toward defeasing old bonds |
| Planned if the bond passes | $0.4086 | that, plus the new debt |

**Floor to ceiling is about 9.6 cents, roughly 30 percent.** Across this household's
$523,500 of taxable value — $209,000 homesteaded, $314,500 not — a cent is $52 a
year, so the gap is about **$500 a year**. Against the rate actually being levied
today the bond's own share stays 3.85 cents, about $202 a year.

**Where the 31 cents comes from.** The 2026 worksheet levies $42,616,423 on
$11,514,840,121 of taxable value, which is exactly $0.3701. Scheduled principal and
interest looks like about $36.1 million: the debt service fund spent $63,000,437 in
FY2025, of which $26.9 million was the June 2025 defeasance, and the Bond Review
Board shows no new debt issued that year. $36.1M over that value is $0.3127. Treat
it as an estimate with a known basis, not the district's number — if the real
schedule is $38M the floor is $0.33 and the gap is 7.9 cents instead of 9.6.

**One caveat the other way.** The $0.4086 is the current levy plus the district's
3.85 cents, so it inherits the same defeasance headroom. A board that passed the
bond and then levied only its minimum would be lower than $0.4086 in the early
years — and higher later, when the new debt service peaks. The floor-to-ceiling
spread is the fair way to see the choice; it is not a prediction of either rate.

#### Not quite: the 30 percent is not the bond-vs-minimum number

Asked October 21, 2026: "so are you saying that the bond passing would raise the
minimum taxes necessary to meet our bond obligations by about 30%?"

**No — about 12 percent, not 30.** The 30 percent compares two different things:
the floor with no bond (about $0.31) against the rate the district says it would
actually levy with the bond ($0.4086). That $0.4086 carries roughly 6 cents the
board levies above the floor **by choice**, to defease old bonds, and the bond is
not what causes those 6 cents.

Minimum to minimum, the bond adds its own 3.85 cents to a floor of about 31 cents:

- Floor, bond fails: about $0.3127.
- Floor plus the new debt: about $0.3512, which is **12.3 percent** above it.
- What the district plans to levy with the bond: $0.4086, 30.7 percent above it.

So three honest sentences: the bond itself raises the *required* minimum by about
**12 percent**; it raises the rate actually being levied today by **10.4 percent**;
and the floor he could have without it is **30 percent** below what would be levied
with it. The last is the real spread between the cheapest no-bond year and the
planned bond year, which is why it was given first — but it is not the cost of the
bond.

**And 12 percent is the early-years figure.** Manor ISD plans to sell the $400
million in series over several years, so the new debt service starts small and
climbs. The district has never said the 3.85 cents is the full eventual impact,
only that the rate "will increase by .0385" if both propositions pass. The
minimum-to-minimum gap widens as the new payments ramp.
