## Supreme Court of Texas, Place 1 — Chief Justice

**Jimmy Blacklock (R), incumbent.** Chief Justice since January 2025, when
Governor Abbott elevated him after Nathan Hecht hit the mandatory judicial
retirement age. Abbott first appointed him to the court in 2018; he won
election to the seat afterward. Yale Law School. Before the bench he was
Abbott's general counsel and worked in the Texas Attorney General's office,
where he handled challenges to the Affordable Care Act and defended Texas
abortion law.

A self-described originalist. Wrote the August 2021 opinion holding that the
Texas Constitution lets each legislative chamber compel absent members to
attend, by physical arrest if needed — the ruling in the fight over House
Democrats leaving the state to break quorum. In late 2024 he wrote a strongly
worded opinion siding with a Texas judge who refused to perform same-sex
weddings on religious grounds.

Ran unopposed in the March 3, 2026 Republican primary.

**Maggie Ellis (D), challenger.** Justice on the Texas Third Court of Appeals
in Austin, elected to it in 2024. Practiced representing individuals before
taking the bench; board certified in juvenile law. Beat Cory Carlyle in the
March 3, 2026 Democratic primary.

## Manor ISD bond, Propositions A and B

Asked October 21, 2026: "how much will the 2026 school district bond cost us above
our baseline debt obligations?"

**On the ballot.** Two propositions, called unanimously by the Manor ISD board for
November 3, 2026.

- **Proposition A, $391.5 million principal.** Manor New Tech High School expansion,
  a Bluebonnet Trail Elementary rebuild, Manor High School renovation and location
  consolidation, a theater addition and an 18+ program facility, renovations at Decker
  and Blake Manor elementaries, districtwide safety and security, new buses. With
  interest, $701,466,608.
- **Proposition B, $8.5 million principal.** Student and staff technology. With
  interest, $8,825,374.

Together $400 million borrowed and **$710.3 million repaid**, of which about
**$310 million is interest**.

**Above the baseline.** Manor ISD already owes $624,834,706 in tax-supported debt,
carried by an I&S (debt service) rate of **$0.3701 per $100**. The board adopted that
rate in August 2026 deliberately above what the existing debt needs, so the surplus
can defease or refund old bonds and cut long-term interest; the 2026-27 total rate is
$1.0755, being $0.7054 M&O plus $0.3701 I&S, down from $1.0814.

The district's own estimate is that passing both propositions **raises the rate by
3.85 cents per $100** — essentially all of it Proposition A, since Proposition B
rounds to zero. That 3.85 cents is the answer to "above baseline": **$38.50 a year
per $100,000 of taxable value**, for as long as the new debt is outstanding.

**What it costs this household**, at the values David carries in YNAB as of October
2026 and at the $140,000 school homestead exemption Texas voters approved in
November 2025:

| | Taxable value | The bond's share |
|---|---|---|
| Home, less the $140,000 homestead exemption | $209,000 | $80 a year |
| Second house, no homestead exemption | $314,500 | $121 a year |
| **Both** | | **$202 a year, about $17 a month** |

Over a thirty-year repayment at today's values that is roughly **$6,000** — more if
appraisals rise, less if they fall.

**Read the district's headline carefully.** Manor ISD advertises a *decrease* of $9.56
a month for the average home, which it puts at $331,764, if both propositions pass.
That is a year-over-year comparison and not the cost of the bond: appraised values fell
this year, and the district nets the fall against the increase. The bond's own cost is
the 3.85 cents, paid whether or not a particular home's appraisal went down. Homeowners
65 and older or disabled who hold the Over-65 exemption, now $200,000, pay nothing more
provided they make no major improvements.

Sources: manorisd.net Bond 2026 (overview, Finances & Estimated Impact, FAQ); Manor ISD
Board Briefs, August 17, 2026; Texas Scorecard, "Manor ISD Asks Taxpayers to Back $710
Million in Bond Debt."

### How much bigger does the bond make the district's debt?

Asked October 21, 2026: "in percentages how much higher is the district's debt
obligation if the bond passes, compared to their current debt obligation of
outstanding bonds? Not what they've been collecting but what they would have had
to collect at a minimum." So this compares obligations — what must be repaid,
principal and interest — and not the I&S revenue the district has been taking in.

| | Principal | Interest | Must be repaid |
|---|---|---|---|
| Outstanding bonds, per the district's own figure | — | — | $624,834,706 |
| Proposition A | $391,500,000 | $309,966,608 | $701,466,608 |
| Proposition B | $8,500,000 | $325,374 | $8,825,374 |
| **After both pass** | | | **$1,335,126,688** |

**The obligation rises about 114 percent** — the new debt alone, $710.3 million,
is 1.14 times everything still owed on every bond outstanding. Put the other way,
the district's total debt obligation slightly more than doubles, to about $1.34
billion. That is why Texas Scorecard writes that Proposition A would "more than
double" the district's tax-funded debt.

**The figure this turns on.** $624,834,706 is the number the district's own
documentation carries for current tax-supported debt, and reading it as principal
plus interest is what makes Scorecard's "more than double" arithmetic work. A
second figure is in circulation — nearly $741 million owed, of which nearly $252
million is interest, which looks like an earlier snapshot of the same obligation
taken before the June 2025 defeasance of $26.9 million and the $41 million of
refunding savings. On that basis the increase is 96 percent rather than 114. The
Texas Bond Review Board's last closed year, FY2025, shows $473,964,999 of
tax-supported principal outstanding and no new debt issued that year.

Either basis gives the same plain answer: **passing the bond roughly doubles what
Manor ISD owes.**

**And a minimum, not a ceiling.** The $310 million of interest is the district's
estimate at the rates it assumes; the ballot language commits only the $400
million of principal, and bonds sold into worse markets cost more. The annual
side, for scale: the 2026 tax rate worksheet puts the debt service owed this year
on existing bonds at $42,616,423, against an I&S levy of $0.3701 per $100 on
about $11.7 billion of certified value.

### The rate as a percentage, and the all-in cost of the $400 million

Asked October 21, 2026: "what's the percentage impact to our tax rate and also
what's the total cost of the $400 million bond after you account for interest and
anything else?"

**The rate.** The 3.85 cents is a **3.58 percent** increase in the Manor ISD tax
rate: $1.0755 to $1.1140 per $100. All of it lands on the debt-service half, so the
I&S rate alone rises **10.40 percent**, $0.3701 to $0.4086. Measured against the
whole property tax bill the share is smaller: his second house paid $9,858 in 2025
on $314,500, a 3.13 percent all-in rate across county, hospital district, college,
MUD and school, and the bond's $121 of that is about **1.2 percent** of the bill.

| Against | Now | After | Increase |
|---|---|---|---|
| Manor ISD total rate | $1.0755 | $1.1140 | 3.58% |
| I&S (debt) rate only | $0.3701 | $0.4086 | 10.40% |
| His whole tax bill | | | about 1.2% |

**The total cost.** $400 million borrowed is **$710.3 million repaid** — $310.3
million of it interest, **1.78 times the principal**, on the district's own
estimates ($701,466,608 on Proposition A and $8,825,374 on Proposition B).

Three things sit outside that number:

- **Issuance costs**, roughly **$3 to $4 million** — underwriter discount, bond
  counsel, municipal advisor, rating fees — are paid out of the proceeds rather than
  added on top, so under $397 million actually reaches the buildings. Manor ISD has
  not published a figure; recent Texas ISD official statements run 0.75 to 1.0
  percent of par (Galena Park 2026, $959,000 on $110 million; Dallas ISD, $3.82
  million on $500 million).
- **The interest is an estimate, not a cap.** The ballot commits only the $400
  million of principal. Bonds sold into higher rates cost more, and the district
  plans to issue in series over years, so the final interest is unknown on election
  day.
- **Operating costs.** New and expanded campuses cost money to staff, cool and
  maintain, and that falls on the M&O rate, which bond money may not pay. Nothing in
  the $710.3 million covers it.

For this household the bond's own share is about $202 a year, roughly $6,000 over a
thirty-year repayment at today's values.

### The I&S rate with the bond, against a true no-bond floor

Asked October 21, 2026: "compare what our I&S rate would be if the bond didn't pass
and we only paid our minimum current debt obligations versus if it did pass."

The comparison he wants is not 0.3701 against 0.4086, because **0.3701 is itself a
choice**. Senate Bill 1453, effective January 1, 2026, makes a district calculate the
*minimum debt service rate* — enough to pay only the bond payments due that year,
defeasance and refinancing excluded — and allows a higher rate only on a specific
motion carried by a 60 percent supermajority. Manor ISD took that vote on August 17,
2026, unanimously, and Trustee Patterson's motion said the excess "will be used for
the payment of bond debt through defeasance or refunding to reduce the long term
interest cost on currently existing debt." The district posted only the
higher-debt-rate worksheet; it has not published the minimum figure.

| | I&S rate per $100 | What it pays for |
|---|---|---|
| Minimum on existing debt, bond fails | **about $0.31** (estimated) | this year's principal and interest, nothing more |
| Adopted now, bond fails | $0.3701 | that, plus about $6.5M a year toward defeasing old bonds |
| Planned if the bond passes | $0.4086 | that, plus the new debt |

**Floor to ceiling is about 9.6 cents, roughly 30 percent.** Across this household's
$523,500 of taxable value — $209,000 homesteaded, $314,500 not — a cent is $52 a
year, so the gap is about **$500 a year**. Against the rate actually being levied
today the bond's own share stays 3.85 cents, about $202 a year.

**Where the 31 cents comes from.** The 2026 worksheet levies $42,616,423 on
$11,514,840,121 of taxable value, which is exactly $0.3701. Scheduled principal and
interest looks like about $36.1 million: the debt service fund spent $63,000,437 in
FY2025, of which $26.9 million was the June 2025 defeasance, and the Bond Review
Board shows no new debt issued that year. $36.1M over that value is $0.3127. Treat
it as an estimate with a known basis, not the district's number — if the real
schedule is $38M the floor is $0.33 and the gap is 7.9 cents instead of 9.6.

**One caveat the other way.** The $0.4086 is the current levy plus the district's
3.85 cents, so it inherits the same defeasance headroom. A board that passed the
bond and then levied only its minimum would be lower than $0.4086 in the early
years — and higher later, when the new debt service peaks. The floor-to-ceiling
spread is the fair way to see the choice; it is not a prediction of either rate.

#### Not quite: the 30 percent is not the bond-vs-minimum number

Asked October 21, 2026: "so are you saying that the bond passing would raise the
minimum taxes necessary to meet our bond obligations by about 30%?"

**No — about 12 percent, not 30.** The 30 percent compares two different things:
the floor with no bond (about $0.31) against the rate the district says it would
actually levy with the bond ($0.4086). That $0.4086 carries roughly 6 cents the
board levies above the floor **by choice**, to defease old bonds, and the bond is
not what causes those 6 cents.

Minimum to minimum, the bond adds its own 3.85 cents to a floor of about 31 cents:

- Floor, bond fails: about $0.3127.
- Floor plus the new debt: about $0.3512, which is **12.3 percent** above it.
- What the district plans to levy with the bond: $0.4086, 30.7 percent above it.

So three honest sentences: the bond itself raises the *required* minimum by about
**12 percent**; it raises the rate actually being levied today by **10.4 percent**;
and the floor he could have without it is **30 percent** below what would be levied
with it. The last is the real spread between the cheapest no-bond year and the
planned bond year, which is why it was given first — but it is not the cost of the
bond.

**And 12 percent is the early-years figure.** Manor ISD plans to sell the $400
million in series over several years, so the new debt service starts small and
climbs. The district has never said the 3.85 cents is the full eventual impact,
only that the rate "will increase by .0385" if both propositions pass. The
minimum-to-minimum gap widens as the new payments ramp.

#### The three numbers side by side

Asked October 21, 2026: "if we didn't pass the bond and we only paid our minimum
existing obligations, our tax rate could go down significantly. What would it go
down to, what will it have to be to support the bond, and what is the percentage
difference between those two?"

Yes — it could go down, and that is the comparison the district's own material
never shows. Both halves of the school rate, with M&O held at $0.7054 because the
bond does not touch it:

| | I&S rate | Manor ISD total rate | This household, per year |
|---|---|---|---|
| Levied today | $0.3701 | $1.0755 | — |
| **Floor, bond fails** | **about $0.3127** | about $1.0181 | **about $300 less** |
| **Floor plus the bond** | **about $0.3512** | about $1.0566 | about **$202 more** than the floor |
| Planned if the bond passes | $0.4086 | $1.1140 | about $502 more than the floor |

**Floor to floor the difference is 12.3 percent** — $0.3127 to $0.3512 — which is
3.8 percent of the total school rate. That is the bond's own number. Floor to the
rate the district says it would actually levy is 30.7 percent, and the extra comes
from the roughly 6 cents of defeasance headroom the board keeps by choice, not from
the bond.

Two cautions on the first column. The $0.3127 is an estimate with a stated basis,
not a Manor ISD figure: the district posted only the higher-debt-rate worksheet, and
a further search on October 21 still found no published minimum. And no board has
promised to levy the floor in either case — this is the span of the choice, not a
forecast of what will be charged.

#### Why doubling the debt only moves the rate a few points

Asked October 21, 2026: "how does doubling our bond obligations only increase our
rate by a few percentage points?"

Because the two numbers measure different things, and because the small one is a
first-year number.

**A stock against a flow.** The $1.34 billion is everything owed across thirty
years. The tax rate pays one year of it. The $710.3 million of new obligation,
spread over a thirty-year repayment, averages about **$23.7 million a year**
against the **$42.6 million** the district levies for debt this year - about **56
percent more**, not 100 percent. Doubling what is owed does not double what is
paid annually: the new money stretches over thirty years, while the existing $625
million, at roughly $40 million a year, runs off in about fifteen.

**But 56 percent is still not 3.6 percent.** At the 2026 taxable value of
$11,514,840,121, one cent of I&S raises about $1.15 million. So $23.7 million of
new annual debt service is about **20.6 cents** - more than five times the 3.85
cents advertised. The 3.85 cents is worth about $4.4 million a year, roughly **19
percent** of the average annual payment the new bonds will require.

Three things close that gap, and none of them is a promise:

- **Staggered issuance.** The district says outright that it will not sell the
  whole $400 million at once, and that staggering is how it "maintains the current
  tax rate." Year one carries one slice; the rate climbs as later series sell.
- **Old debt rolling off.** New series are shaped to fill the space left as the
  existing payments decline. The squeeze is the overlap years, roughly five to
  twelve out, when the new debt is near full service and the old is not yet gone.
- **A growing tax base.** The same dollars cost fewer cents on a bigger roll. This
  is the leg to watch, because Manor's base has not grown lately: $11.68 billion in
  2024 against $11.51 billion in 2026, flat to down, partly the $140,000 homestead
  exemption. Every projection holding the rate near 3.85 cents assumes growth
  resumes.

**And the headroom.** The board already levies about $6.5 million a year above the
minimum, to defease old bonds - about 5.6 cents, more than the whole advertised
increase. A board that stopped defeasing could absorb the first years of new debt
service with no rate change at all, which is part of why "no tax rate increase" is
sayable at all.

**The short answer.** It doesn't, for long. The 3.85 cents is what the first slice
costs on a base the district hopes will grow. The full $400 million, at today's
base, needs about 20 cents.

#### Year 1 against the full impact, and when the full impact lands

Asked October 21, 2026: "so wait, you're telling me they're giving us year 1 costs
but not the full impact? And what year do we start feeling the full impact?"

**Yes.** Manor ISD's own words are that the rate "will increase by .0385" if both
propositions pass. It has never called that the life-of-bond figure, and it says
plainly that it will not sell the $400 million at once — staggering the sales is
how it says it "maintains the current tax rate." A rate that pays one slice of the
borrowing is a first-years rate by construction.

**No issuance schedule is published.** Searched again on October 21: the FAQ says
only that bonds can be sold 45 to 60 days after the election is canvassed, when
market conditions are good, and that construction begins several months after the
election. No series, no years, no dollar amounts per series.

**So the year is an estimate.** A $400 million program of this shape — a rebuild,
two major renovations, an expansion, buses and technology — is normally built over
four to five years and sold in three or four series, each series adding a full year
of debt service the year after it sells.

| | Estimated |
|---|---|
| First series sold | early 2027, first full year of its debt service 2028 |
| Last series sold | about 2031–2032 |
| First year all $400 million is in service | **about 2033 to 2035**, six to eight years out |
| What full service costs at the 2026 base | about **20.6 cents**, vs 3.85 advertised |

**The squeeze is wider than the ramp.** Roughly **2032 through 2038** is the stretch
where the new debt is near full service and the existing $625 million has not
finished running off. After that the old payments are gone and the new ones have the
rate to themselves, which is cheaper — if the tax base has grown. It has not lately:
$11.68 billion in 2024 against $11.51 billion in 2026.

These years are inference, not a district figure. What is not inference: the 3.85
cents buys the first slice, and nobody has published what the last slice costs.

#### What Manor ISD owes now

Asked October 22, 2026: "tell me about the school district current outstanding
balances." Three different balances get quoted, which is why the numbers in
circulation disagree.

| Balance | Amount | Basis |
|---|---|---|
| Principal still owed | **$473,964,999** | Texas Bond Review Board, FY2025 close, August 31 2025 |
| Principal plus remaining interest | **$624,834,706** | the district's own figure for current tax-supported debt |
| Interest inside that | about **$151 million** | the difference between the two rows above |
| An older snapshot still quoted | about **$741M**, incl. **$252M** interest | pre-dates the June 2025 defeasance and the refunding |
| Owed this year | **$42,616,423** | 2026 tax rate worksheet, I&S levy of $0.3701 per $100 |

Of this year's $42.6 million, about **$36.1 million** is scheduled principal and
interest; the remaining **$6.5 million** is the board paying ahead to defease old
bonds, which it chooses and could stop.

**No new borrowing since.** The Bond Review Board shows $0 of new issuance in
FY2025, tax-supported and revenue-supported alike, and $0 of revenue-supported
debt outstanding. In June 2025 the district defeased $26.9 million and took about
$41 million of refunding savings, which is what separates the $625M figure from
the older $741M one.

**Runoff.** At roughly $40 million a year the existing balance clears in about
fifteen years — an estimate, since the district does not publish its amortization
schedule. The bond would add $710.3 million of obligation on top of it, which is
the doubling.

#### If the bond fails and the rate holds: paid off about 2039

Asked October 22, 2026: "if we don't pass the bond and we keep paying at the current
rate, defeasing debt early, how soon will the outstanding debt be completely cleared?"

**About thirteen years — around FY2039.** The run-off estimate above (fifteen years at
roughly $40 million) did not credit the early payoff. Counting it:

| Step | Amount |
|---|---|
| Owed, principal plus remaining interest | **$624.8M** |
| Levied a year at the adopted $0.3701 | **$42.6M** |
| — of which scheduled principal and interest | $36.1M |
| — of which paid ahead to defease | $6.5M |
| Future interest killed per $1 defeased | about **$0.50** |
| Obligation removed per year | about **$45.9M** |
| Already in the debt service fund (Aug 31, 2025) | **$48.2M** |

$624.8M at $45.9M a year is 13.6 years; the $48.2M already banked is about another
year of payments, which brings it to roughly **twelve or thirteen years, FY2039**.

**The 50 cents is measured, not assumed.** The August 18, 2025 board brief: "During the
month of June, we defeased a total of $26.9MM with a total savings of $13.4MM in
long-term interest." That same brief shows the Debt Service Fund collecting $48,787,353
and spending $59,895,910 in FY2024-25, ending at a $48,249,449 balance.

**Stopping the defeasance costs four years.** Paying only the scheduled $36.1 million
takes about seventeen years, to **2043**, and about **$42 million** more in interest —
the mirror image of the point in *Why the rate barely moves*: the $6.5 million of
headroom the board levies by choice is both what could absorb the new bond's early
years and what is currently buying four years off the old debt. It cannot do both.

**Still an estimate.** Dollars against dollars, not a maturity table: the district
publishes no amortization schedule, some series likely mature into the 2040s, only
callable or escrowable bonds can be retired ahead, and no growth in the taxable base is
credited ($11.51B in 2026 against $11.68B in 2024). Base growth would pull the date in.

#### Why fifteen, seventeen and thirteen are not inconsistent

Asked October 22, 2026: "also on the bond, you said it clears in 15 years. Then you
said that defeasing saves 4 years and it clears in 13. That doesn't add up for me."

He is right that 15 minus 4 is not 13. The fault is in the two sections above: three
numbers drawn from three different dollars-per-year assumptions, written as though
they shared one.

| Scenario | Counted per year | Clears in |
|---|---|---|
| Minimum only — board stops defeasing | $36.1M scheduled | about **17 years**, 2043 |
| Adopted rate held, dollars counted only | $42.6M levied | about **15 years** |
| Adopted rate held, interest killed and cash on hand counted | $45.9M effective, less $48.2M already banked | about **13 years**, FY2039 |

**Fifteen and thirteen are the same scenario**, not two. Both hold the adopted $0.3701
rate and both keep defeasing. The thirteen only counts two things the fifteen left
out: the roughly 50 cents of future interest that dies with every dollar paid ahead,
and the $48.2 million already sitting in the debt service fund. Thirteen supersedes
fifteen, and the "roughly $40 million a year" in *Runoff* above was loose — the levy is
$42.6M, which on dollars alone is 14.7 years.

**The four years is seventeen against thirteen** — what the $6.5 million of defeasance
the board levies by choice buys, measured against stopping it. It was never fifteen
against thirteen, and no answer above claimed defeasance turns 15 into 13.

## The whole ballot, and every option on it

Asked October 24, 2026: "research my ballot and let me know what's on it, work up
each of the options to vote for." (The ring heard "my account.")

**His ballot is the Travis County consolidated ballot filtered to:** Congressional
District 10 (the new 2025 map), Texas House District 46, State Board of Education
District 5, Justice of the Peace Precinct 1, County Commissioner Precinct 1 (not up
this year), the Austin Community College taxing district, Manor ISD and the City of
Manor. About **forty contests**, of which about **twenty are a real choice** - the

runs to 2028 even though she is running for Comptroller.

The one thing that cannot be settled without his address is whether a **municipal
utility district** board seat appears; he pays a MUD on the second house and MUD
director races are drawn by district. The personalized ballot at votetravis.gov
(Voter Lookup) is the authority on that. Nothing below is advice on how to vote; it
is what each option is.

### U.S. Senate

For the seat John Cornyn has held since 2002. Polls have it a dead heat: Texas
Public Opinion Research on October 23 had Talarico 51-47; Fox News had 49-49; the UT
Texas Politics Project had Talarico 50-47. No debate has been agreed to.

- **Ken Paxton (R).** Attorney General since 2015. Impeached by the Texas House in
  2023 and acquitted by the Senate; securities-fraud charges dismissed in 2024 after
  nine years; the whistleblower suit by his own former deputies is unresolved.
  Running on the border, the Trump agenda and control of the chamber.
- **James Talarico (D).** State representative from Austin, former middle-school
  teacher, seminary student, large online following. Running an anti-corruption
  campaign aimed at Paxton and at Elon Musk's money in Texas politics.
- **Ted Brown (L).** Longtime Austin-area Libertarian activist.

### Governor

Abbott leads by about 3 to 4 points in the October polling averages, and leads voters
45 and older while Hinojosa leads the under-45s. He ended the September 24 reporting
period with $18.2 million on hand against her $4 million.

- **Greg Abbott (R).** Governor since 2015, seeking a fourth term. Chief champion of
  the education savings account (voucher) program passed in 2025.
- **Gina Hinojosa (D).** State representative from Austin, former Austin ISD board
  president. Would end the voucher program and raise public school funding; the rest
  of the campaign is affordability, health care costs and insurance.
- **Pat Dixon (L).** Former Lago Vista council member, long-time Libertarian
  candidate.

### Lieutenant Governor

The office that controls what the Texas Senate votes on. One October poll had
Goodwin narrowly ahead; an October 17 poll had Patrick up 7.6.

- **Dan Patrick (R).** Lieutenant Governor since 2015, seeking a fourth term; drove
  vouchers and the THC ban through the Senate.
- **Vikki Goodwin (D).** State representative from west Austin, real estate broker.
- **Anthony Cristo (L)** and **Kevin McCormick (G)** are also on the ballot.

### Attorney General

An open seat, and the closest statewide race after the Senate: polling averages have
Middleton 46, Johnson 43, with more than a fifth undecided.

- **Mayes Middleton (R).** State senator from Galveston, oil and gas businessman and
  heavy self-funder. Promises to enforce the Trump deportation orders, defend the
  abortion laws and fight what he calls the radical gender agenda.
- **Nathan Johnson (D).** State senator from Dallas, lawyer with a reputation for

## Land Office and Agriculture, worked up at length

Asked October 24, 2026: "in the ballot recap expander, add session sections about the
Commissioner of the General Land Office and the Commissioner of Agriculture." Those two
races got three bullets each in the ballot section above, the thinnest on the whole
ballot; this is both of them at the length the rest got.

### Commissioner of the General Land Office

**The office.** The oldest agency in Texas, created in 1836. It manages about **13
million acres** of state land and the mineral rights under it, and the leasing revenue
feeds the **Permanent School Fund** - $57 billion in assets, about **$2.4 billion a year
to public schools**, which is why this office matters to a Manor ISD household even
though it never appears on a tax bill. It also runs the federal disaster recovery money
Texas receives, the **Veterans Land Board** (land and home loans, state veterans homes
and cemeteries), coastal protection, and **the Alamo**. Four-year term.

**Dawn Buckingham (R), incumbent.** Ophthalmic surgeon from Lakeway, state senator for
District 24 before this, the first woman to hold the office; elected 2022, unopposed in
the March 3, 2026 primary. Her record as she runs on it: nearly **$6 billion** sent to
public education and about **$9 billion** of disaster recovery administered during the
term, energy development expanded on state land, and new exploration of geothermal,
small modular nuclear and natural gas leasing. She names **water** as the biggest
five-year problem facing Texas and backs research into treating produced water from oil
and gas wells. On the border she has bought ranches near Big Bend for state control,
launched the **Jocelyn Initiative** on immigration enforcement, and ordered federal
contractors off state border land; she promises to "hold anyone accountable who enters,
damages or alters Texas property without authorization." The **Alamo** is her one
sustained controversy: she called the site recognition of Indigenous Peoples Day "woke"
and Alamo Trust chief executive Kate Rogers was forced out after the backlash.
Endorsed by Trump, 11 state senators, 42 state representatives and the land and energy
industry groups. **$3.2 million** cash on hand at June 30.

**Benjamin Flores (D), challenger.** Bay City city councilman on the Matagorda coast,
thirty years in cybersecurity and compliance, served on local economic development
boards, raises pigs on a small scale; an immigrant from Mexico; worked community
disaster response after the Gulf storms. Beat veteran and labor organizer Jose Loya in
the March primary. Platform: publish **timelines and dashboards** for disaster recovery
and work directly with communities so rural, coastal and low-income Texans are not left
waiting years; expand responsible leasing but strengthen oversight of the Permanent
School Fund; make the Veterans Land Board easier to actually use; coastal and climate
resilience as the first priority; protect academic freedom in how the Alamo is
presented; and he opposes the voucher program. On border security he says Texas can
protect the border "without surrendering control of state lands or sacrificing parks,
resources and private property rights." In late May 2026 his campaign disclosed a
**leukemia** diagnosis; two weeks later, in June, he said he was in remission and
stayed in the race. Endorsed by Democratic state representatives, the Texas AFL-CIO,
the Sierra Club and the state party groups. **$3,001** cash on hand - effectively no
money against her $3.2 million.

**Neill Snider (L).** Mechanical contractor from Waco. Wants lower taxes, the land run
for maximum profit and more transparency at the GLO. $0 raised; did not answer the
Community Impact candidate questionnaire.

**Polling, and it does not agree with itself.** University of Houston, October 10-14:
**Buckingham 47, Flores 40**, Snider 2, 11 undecided. Texas Public Opinion Research,
October 15-18: **49-48**. ActiVote, October 17: **51.9-48.1**. One to seven points
depending on the pollster - a thinly polled race where the undecided share is larger
than any lead.

### Commissioner of Agriculture

**The office.** Head of the Texas Department of Agriculture and the chief advocate for
the industry inside state government. It regulates pesticides, plants, grain, cattle
and the hemp business; runs **weights and measures** - the gas pumps and grocery scales
and scanners that have to charge what they say; administers the **school lunch and
breakfast programs**, so Manor ISD cafeterias answer to this office; and delivers rural
health, rural development grants and producer disaster relief. Four-year term. Texas law
also requires a candidate to have been **in the agricultural business for five of the
last ten years**, which is the fight this race turned into.

**Nate Sheets (R).** Co-founded **Nature Nate's** raw honey with his wife and sold the
company in 2021; six years in the Naval Reserve; once communications director for an
evangelical ministry. Also holds Nature Nate's Farms, bought in 2012, and a
**10,000-acre ranch near Guthrie** with cattle and cutting horses. **He beat three-term
incumbent Sid Miller in the March 3 primary** - Abbott endorsed Sheets in January, Trump
had endorsed Miller, and Miller has pointedly not endorsed Sheets since. Platform: clean
and healthy food for Texas schools and harsh chemicals out of the food supply, which
places him with the **MAHA** movement around Robert F. Kennedy Jr.; profitability
restored for farmers and ranchers; **against tax breaks for data centers**; citizenship
verification for employment; federal agroterrorism investigations. Raised **$1.9
million** January to June (Jim Moyer $300,000, the Texas Republican Leadership Fund
$294,600) with $104,510 on hand at June 30 - he spends it as it comes. Endorsed by
Abbott, three state senators and Jim McIngvale.

**Clayton Tucker (D).** 35, raised on the family ranch in Lampasas, fifth-generation
Texan like Sheets and out of the same Hill Country; fair trade organizer, water and
agriculture researcher for the National Science Foundation, now secretary of the **Texas
Farmers Union**. Platform: keep **data centers off farmland** through local "Ag
Development Districts"; lower food costs and break up the corporate monopolies in meat
and grain; regulate PFAS and microplastics and get chemicals out of food; back the hemp
farmers; rebuild agricultural cooperatives so young farmers can start. Raised
**$264,673** with $162,456 on hand (Don Henley gave $50,000). Endorsed by Lloyd Doggett,
Greg Casar and the Texas AFL-CIO.

**Austin R. Kelly (L).** Born in Comanche County, runs an ecological consulting firm,
formerly lectured in rangeland and botany at Texas A&M. Wants the red tape cut off hemp,
cottage food and local meat processing, and opposes licensing schemes, warrantless
inspections and eminent domain abuse. $494 raised.

**Each says the other is ineligible.** Tucker filed a complaint arguing that the honey
company does not satisfy the five-of-ten-years rule - "this is about following the Texas

## State Board of Education, District 5 - the long version

Added October 24, 2026, when he asked for more about this race. The short entry
above stands; this is the whole of it.

### What the seat actually controls

Fifteen elected members, four-year terms, no pay. The board does four things that
reach into every public and charter classroom in Texas:

1. **The standards (TEKS).** What must be taught in every subject at every grade.
   These drive the STAAR tests and, through them, district A-F ratings.
2. **Instructional materials.** The board reviews and approves the textbooks and
   curriculum products the state pays for, and since 2023 it approves the
   state-developed materials districts are paid a bonus to adopt.
3. **Charter schools.** The board can veto a new charter chain the commissioner
   has approved - three of fifteen votes short of a veto and the charter opens.
4. **The Permanent School Fund.** The board sets the investment policy for the
   fund (about $57B) and the annual distribution to schools; the Land Office
   manages the land side of it. The same fund appears on his ballot under the
   Land Office race.

What the board does **not** control: vouchers and ESAs (the Legislature and the
Comptroller), teacher pay, school finance, district operations, library books in
a particular district, or the commissioner of education (the governor appoints
him). A candidate promising to fix teacher pay from this seat is promising
something the seat cannot do.

The board is **11 Republicans and 4 Democrats** going into this election, and
**eight of the fifteen seats are up**, so the November result sets the board that
writes standards for the next four years.

### Why this seat is open

**Rebecca Bell-Metereau (D)** has held District 5 since January 2021 - she took it
from Ken Mercer, one of the most socially conservative members the board ever had,
and she is the retiring member now, not seeking a third term. She won re-election
in 2022 by **63.8% to 36.2%**, about 28 points, which is the number to keep in
mind: District 5 is Travis-anchored and has voted Democratic for this seat by wide
margins. An open seat in a district like that is a Democratic hold on paper.

District 5 is Travis, Hays, Bastrop, Caldwell and Guadalupe counties plus parts of
Blanco and Williamson - Austin, Manor, San Marcos, Seguin, Bastrop, Kyle, Buda,
Lockhart.

### The board's recent record, which is what the race is about

- **Bluebonnet Learning**, the state-written K-5 reading and math curriculum the
  board approved in November 2024 on an 8-7 vote, with Bible-based lessons woven
  through the reading units (the Sermon on the Mount in a kindergarten unit, the
  Golden Rule, the Last Supper in a unit on art). Districts that adopt it get $60
  per student. It has since needed a **$8.4 million correction contract** for
  thousands of errors found after adoption - the number both campaigns mean when
  they say the board wastes money.
- **Social studies standards** rewritten with a heavier Christian and Texan
  emphasis, after the 2022 rewrite collapsed.
- **An approved reading list** criticised in and out of the board for how little of
  it is by or about anyone who is not white.
- **Charter votes** that have split the board, and a running argument about whether
  the board should be rubber-stamping approvals from the commissioner.

### Dolores "Mica" Arellano (R)

- **Who.** A thirty-year educator, lives in Kyle (Hays County). Her current work is
  listed as a client advocate at a Hays County agency. No prior office; this is her
  first run. Unopposed in the March 3 primary, 78,523 votes.
- **The platform**, which she calls the Five-Star Education Standard: school safety
  ("secure, structured classroom environments"), proven academics ("strong,
  measurable curriculum standards aimed at academic and career preparation"),
  teacher support (reducing burnout, giving educators the tools they need),
  parental voice (parents as partners in decisions), and responsible spending (tax
  dollars to "students and classrooms rather than unnecessary bureaucracy").
- **What is not said.** Her site and her voter-guide entries carry no position on
  Bluebonnet, on the Bible-infused lessons, on charter expansion, on the Permanent
  School Fund, or on vouchers. She **declined KUT's interview request** and left
  the iVoterGuide questionnaire blank - every item, not only the education ones.
  Her own site's testimonials section still holds placeholder text. So the honest
  summary is a career teacher running on competence and classroom experience, with
  the contested questions unanswered in public.
- **Endorsements.** Travis County Republican Party. iVoterGuide rates her
  "Conservative" on her party and giving history rather than on answers.
- **Money.** Nothing filed beyond self-funding on record - her reported
  contributions are from herself.

### Allison Bush (D)

- **Who.** Twenty-five years in Texas public education - classroom teacher, career
  counselor, administrator, nonprofit leader. Theater, speech and career-technical
  classes in Crosby ISD early on, later Pflugerville and Bastrop. Was a special
  education student herself and has dyslexia, which she talks about as the reason
  she teaches the way she does. First run for office.
- **How she got here.** Led a six-candidate March primary with 83,648 votes
  (35.5%), then won the May 26 runoff over Stephanie Limon Bazan **61.2% to 38.8%**.
- **The platform.** The board's job is to set strong standards and a reasonable
  curriculum framework and then stop - "transparent standards, responsible
  governance and student-focused policies reflecting the needs of all communities,
  not political agendas." Specifically: historically accurate and varied
  curriculum, no faith-specific teaching mandates, no reading lists teachers
  cannot realistically use, **more district latitude in how to meet state
  standards** (her argument is that Texas is too varied for one prescribed route),
  educator input weighted heavily in standards and materials decisions, charter
  accountability before charter expansion, and board process published in plain
  language with real timelines. Her stated reason for running is that the board
  has too few members who have taught.
- **Endorsements.** Austin American-Statesman editorial board (recommending her
  for a nonpartisan approach that puts students and teachers before political
  agendas), Austin Chronicle, Austin Central Labor Council (she is a former Texas
  AFT member), Asian American Democrats of Texas, Liberal Austin Democrats, and a
  number of local school board members.
- **Money.** No large totals reported; this is a low-dollar race on both sides.

### How to read the choice

Two career educators, no incumbent, and a race that turns on **how far the state
should specify what is taught**. Bush has said plainly what she would undo - the
Bible-infused reading units, the prescriptive materials, the board's political
drift - and would widen district latitude. Arellano is running on safety,
measurable standards, parental voice and spending discipline, and has not said in
public where she lands on the Bluebonnet-era decisions, which is the one thing a
voter would most want from her.

There is **no Libertarian or write-in** in this race: it is a two-way. A seat the
Democratic incumbent held by 28 points, open, with eight of fifteen board seats on
ballots across Texas the same night.

## Travis Central Appraisal District, Place 3 - the long version

Added October 24, 2026, when he asked for more about this race. The short entry above
stands; this is the whole of it. It is the one contested seat on his ballot that bears
directly on the two houses he pays appraised taxes on.

### What the seat actually controls

The 2023 property tax law (SB 2) rebuilt appraisal district boards in counties over
75,000: **nine members** - five appointed by the taxing units that fund the district
(the county, the city, the school districts), **three elected countywide**, and the
county tax assessor-collector as an ex-officio director. Places 1, 2 and 3 are those
three elected seats, on a **nonpartisan** ballot.

What the board does: **hires and fires the chief appraiser**, approves TCAD's budget
and contracts, and sets general policy. What it does not do: appraise property. No
director can change his appraisal, and nobody on this ballot can lower his tax rate -
that is the city, the county, Manor ISD and ACC.

**The one power that reaches his tax bill is the Appraisal Review Board.** The ARB is
the citizen panel that hears protests, and under SB 2 the TCAD board appoints it. The
appointment needs a majority of the board **and at least two of the three elected
directors have to be in that majority** - so these three seats hold a practical veto
over who hears protests. That is the whole case for caring about this race.

**Timing.** The winner takes office **January 1, 2027**. The three elected directors
then **draw lots**: one serves two years, two serve four, and every elected term after
that is four years. So the Place 3 winner may be back on the ballot in 2028 or in 2030,
decided by chance.

**The elected bloc turns over completely.** The three elected in May 2024 - Don Hanna,
Shenghao Wang and Dick Lavine - hold terms that expire December 31, 2026, and none of
them is on this ballot. Four people filed for the three seats; three are Democrats and
one a Republican, and only the two in Place 3 own homes. Places 1 (**Skyler Korgel**,
32) and 2 (**Brian Pena**, 23) are unopposed, which makes Place 3 the only vote he
casts for this board.

### Yvonne Munoz

**Chief of staff to state Rep. Vincent Perez (D-El Paso)**, who sits on the Texas House
**Ways and Means Committee** - the committee that writes property tax and appraisal law.
Before that she was legislative director for Rep. Eddie Morales (D-Eagle Pass) through
the 2023 session, the session that produced SB 2 and this very board. Over a decade
advising elected officials and public-sector leaders in Texas and California; a master's
degree focused on **measurement and assessment**, and a consulting practice doing data
analysis and program evaluation; graduate of the LBJ Women's Campaign School.

A **first-time homeowner** - a 1,275 square-foot house off US 183 in north Austin, the
smaller of the two candidates' homes by half.

**What she runs on:** making property tax information findable and understandable - how
homestead exemptions work, how a protest is filed, what the numbers on the notice mean.
Her read of the problem is that the system is navigable only by people who already know
how to navigate it. It is an access and transparency platform, not a spending fight.

**Endorsements:** the **Austin Chronicle**, the **Austin Central Labor Council**, and
roughly thirteen Democratic clubs and voter guides. The Chronicle called both candidates
"well-versed for the position given their professional experiences" and gave her "a
slight leg up" purely on the Ways and Means proximity.

### Philip Gorman

A consultant in **"cloud economics"** - cutting companies' cloud computing bills; he
says he monitors and optimizes tens of millions of dollars of cost a year. **Treasurer
of the Circle C Homeowners Association**, a 5,600-home master-planned development in
Oak Hill, where he oversees a **$7 million** annual budget. Owns a 2,455 square-foot
Circle C home TCAD values at **$684,000**.

**What he runs on:** watching the appraisal district's own spending closely, and one
sharp line about appraisal itself - *"It's a money transfer from those who don't protest
to those who do."* He campaigned against **Austin's 2025 tax rate increase** and against
**Proposition Q**, the permanent five-cent-per-$100 city rate increase, making the point
that it was permanent rather than temporary; he notes Circle C voted four to one against
it. Worth knowing that Prop Q was a **City of Austin** question - Manor is outside the
city limits, so it never touched David's bill either way.

**Endorsements:** none listed by any organization.

### How to read it

The honest summary is that both are competent and neither is running on anything
alarming, which is why this comes down to which half of the problem he wants addressed.

Gorman's line is the one that names David's actual grievance: unequal appraisal is a
transfer from the people who do not protest to the people who do, and the board's real
lever on that is who sits on the ARB. Munoz's answer to the same unfairness is to make
protesting legible to everyone instead of only to those who already know - which, since
David does protest, helps his neighbours more than it helps him.

- **For the budget hawk and an ARB inclined to take protests seriously: Gorman.** He
  has the governance record (a $7 million HOA budget, a 5,600-home association) and he
  is the only one of the two who has said anything about protest outcomes.
- **For fluency in the law that sets the rules: Munoz.** She works inside the committee
  that writes appraisal law, which is a real advantage in a job whose main constraint is
  statute, and she is the consensus pick of every organization that weighed in.

Either way the effect on his bill is indirect and slow, and the rate-setting bodies -
Manor ISD above all - move his taxes far more than this seat does.

## Austin Community College trustees - the long version

Added October 24, 2026, when he asked for more about the contested ACC seats. The
short entry above stands; this is the whole of it. **Two of the three seats are a
choice** - Place 1 and Place 3. Place 2 (Gigi Edwards Bryant) is unopposed.

Trustees are elected **at large across the whole taxing district** - eight school
districts across six counties plus the City of Austin - on a **nonpartisan** ballot,
for **six-year terms**. Manor ISD is inside the district, so he votes both races and
pays the rate. Six years means whoever wins is still seated in 2032.

### What is actually in front of this board

- **Free tuition is the whole question.** The pilot launched August 2024 and covers
  the graduating classes of 2024 through 2028 from district high schools. Year two
  reached **nearly 10,000 students** (up from 4,894), about **24% of enrollment**, at
  roughly **$27 million a year**. It **expires after the spring 2028 graduates** -
  well inside the term being voted on. The trustees elected now decide whether it
  becomes permanent, narrows, or simply lapses.
- **The money got tighter this year.** The board approved a **$583 million FY2027
  budget** in July 2026: tuition frozen for the **13th straight year**, a 2% raise
  plus a one-time $1,700 for full-time staff (employees had asked for 4%). ACC lost
  **$6 million** in state money under the new dynamic funding formulas, took its
  **first drop in property tax revenue in 17 years**, and for the first time since
  2014 salaries exceeded property tax revenue.
- **The rate he pays** is about **$0.1034 per $100** - one of the lowest community
  college tax rates in Texas, and about a tenth of what the Manor ISD bond would add
  at full issue. Both incumbents run on having held it flat.
- **The Pflugerville annexation question is not his** (PfISD voters only), but it is
  the board's biggest pending change. PfISD rejected it in 2018, 56.5% against. For
  residents it would cost about $391 a year on an average home and remove the
  **$201-per-credit-hour** out-of-district fee - about $12,000 over a 60-hour degree.

### Place 1: Dana Walker (incumbent) v Gloria Gonzales-Dholakia

**Dana Walker**, trustee since 2020 and current board secretary. Retired from
technology, private-sector and federal government consulting, including eight years
on internet safety at **Meta**; master's in public policy from the Lee Kuan Yew
School, B.A. in international relations from Lehigh. Calls herself the board's
technology expert. Her record as she puts it: approved the free tuition pilot, raised
completion rates, **$38.5 million** to student support services, expanded veteran
services, raised employee wages. Next term: **make free tuition permanent** - but
"I think first we need to be stable financially with what we have" - student AI and
technology fluency, wraparound supports at every campus, more ways to trim the
budget, workforce partnerships with employers like Tesla. Endorsed by the
**Austin American-Statesman**, which called her ACC experience the reason.

**Gloria Gonzales-Dholakia**, Ph.D., **eight years (two terms) on the Leander ISD
board**, vice president of alumni and public policy at the **Jeremiah Program**
(which helps single mothers through college), former ACC adjunct and St. Edward's
education instructor, first-generation college graduate. Her frame is completion
rather than headcount: *"Success is a degree, it's a university transfer, it's a
certification."* Priorities: student-parents and first-generation students
(childcare, transportation, flexible scheduling, nonprofit partnerships), and much
deeper **dual-credit partnerships with the school districts** - which she argues is
both access and a revenue answer to the shortfall. Wants governance that is
community-centred, "not just from the dais." On AI: use the tools, keep the human
advisers. Endorsed by the **Austin Chronicle**, **ACC's own faculty union (ACC AFT)
and Texas AFT**, and a range of city, county and state officials.

**The split is real.** The Statesman went with the incumbent's institutional
knowledge and tech grounding; the Chronicle went with the K-12-plus-higher-ed
background and the breadth of local endorsements. The college's **own faculty union
endorsing against a sitting trustee** is the sharpest signal in either race.

### Place 3: Nan McRaven (incumbent) v Trevor Hale

**Nan McRaven**, on the board **since 2002** - 24 years, four terms, now board chair,
seeking a fifth. Doctorate in community college leadership; owner of McRaven
Consulting; previously vice president of communications at Motorola, **Travis County
budget director**, a Gates Foundation adviser, and vice president of the Greater
Austin Chamber. Record: ACC grew from **6 campuses to 12** under her tenure, two bond
elections passed, Lockhart added to the district, and she helped roll out the free
tuition pilot. Priorities: keep tuition and taxes flat, **sustain the pilot through
community support or an endowment**, make student resources easier to navigate, build
out automotive, manufacturing and healthcare programs, and get the Legislature to
earmark community college funding. *"We want to create the college of the future."*
Endorsed by **both the Statesman and the Chronicle** - the Statesman on institutional
knowledge during a leadership transition, the Chronicle on 25 years of knowing how
the board works while enrollment has risen.

**Trevor Schuyler Hale**, Ph.D., retired professor of business analytics and
administration - University of Houston-Downtown, Texas A&M, Huston-Tillotson - and a
former scientist for the Office of Naval Research; TRS Rule of 80 retiree. Running to
"pay it forward to Austin and Texas volunteering at something I know a thing or two
about." Three distinctive ideas: **trustee term limits** - *"30 years on the board is
something that shouldn't happen"*; **renaming and rebranding** the college with "more
gravitas" to raise its profile and pull in out-of-area enrollment; and **changing free
tuition to apply only after a student finishes the first two years**, so students have
"skin in the game." Also runs on transparency, removing logistical and financial
barriers, and communicating ACC's community impact better. No endorsements found.

**The question in Place 3** is whether 24 years is institutional memory arriving
exactly when it is needed - a new president, the pilot's expiry, a revenue squeeze -
or an argument against itself. Hale is the only candidate on either ACC race proposing
to **narrow** free tuition, and he concedes he is up against a competent incumbent;
his headline idea, a rename, is the kind of thing a single trustee cannot deliver.
